## Social Gradients in Gotham 
This is a work of fiction based on popular educational or psychological theories. The students in this work are purely fictional. Background characters and settings in Gotham provide a familiar context for the difficulties experienced by many modern families. 

This work was written with Claude AI

### The Theories:
#### Habitus - Bourdieu (1986)
The concept of habitus is derived from the work of Bourdieu (1986) and is used in research, particularly within critical socio-political theory, to understand how individuals relate to their social and cultural context.
A summary of habitus based on the sources is as follows:
1. Definition and Components: Habitus is essentially defined as an individual’s world view. It refers to their values, preferences, and ways of interacting. This concept includes an individual’s dispositions, aspirations, and a whole range of ritual practices that are specifically valued within the home and the community.
2. Contextual Relationship (Field): Habitus operates in connection with the external field or social milieu where individuals engage in interaction and resource exchange. Both habitus and this field are important for understanding families’ experiences, as habitus relates to subjective elements while the field relates to objective elements.
3. Educational Implications: Habitus is closely related to cultural capital. It is a tool used to understand how children from different geographic and socio-economic areas utilise their cultural capital, which is tied to their local communities, when creating and responding to texts. Critically, schools frequently operate by adopting the habitus of the dominant group as the natural and proper habitus, thereby treating all students as if they possess equal access to that specific world view

- Hill, S. (2010). The millennium generation: Teacher-researchers exploring new forms of literacy. Journal of Early Childhood Literacy, 10(3), 314–340. https://doi.org/10.1177/1468798410372820

#### Social Gradients 
What are social gradients?
They’re the consistent, system-wide pattern where more socioeconomic advantage → higher average achievement (e.g., reading). The steeper the gradient in a system, the less equitable it is.
How they show up (macro → classroom):
Macro: Countries differ in steepness; steeper = outcomes track wealth more tightly.
Classroom: Schools privilege the dominant group’s cultural capital/habitus (ways of speaking, knowing, behaving). Students whose home literacies align with school norms start ahead; others are framed as “behind.” Resource gaps (food, time, tech, stable routines) and “hidden curriculum” (public performance, tidy materials = “good student”) quietly widen differences.
Impact on learning & wellbeing:
Learning: Earlier vocabulary/knowledge gaps compound; access to devices, books, calm spaces, and timely feedback shapes what students can actually learn and show.
Wellbeing: Chronic material stress (hunger, housing, healthcare) heightens shame and reactivity, lowers attention and stamina, and increases conflict risk—often misread as individual deficit rather than access.

- Freebody, P. (2007). Literacy Education in School: Research perspectives from the past, for the future. Australian Education Review. https://research.acer.edu.au/aer/1

### Student Profiles:

#### Thomas Flint

Strengths: When calm, Thomas shows real pride in his work—he sits up, tracks instructions, and contributes thoughtfully. He’s quick-witted, socially perceptive, and can be protective of younger kids (especially his little brother). He focuses well when he feels respected and has a clear role.
Weaknesses: He has a short fuse and, when he feels slighted, can become rude or deliberately provocative (a “get-even” vibe). Public correction or embarrassment can trigger targeted behavior and classroom disruption. He also has ongoing toileting issues that worsen under stress; he won’t self-initiate bathroom breaks and needs prompting.
Home Life: Lives in Gotham’s Otisburg (Tricorner Yards–adjacent) with a largely absent dad and an overworked mum juggling cleaning/porter shifts. Money and routines are unstable (prepay power, patchy hot water, thin pantry), and mornings are often rushed without breakfast. His younger brother has dwarfism; Thomas is protective and sensitive to fairness around him. Building conditions (mold, outages) and neighborhood pressures (noise, freight traffic) add to day-to-day stress, which shows up in school as hunger, fatigue, and a hair-trigger for dignity threats.

#### Henry Point 
Strengths: Henry is a bright, precocious six-year-old who reads, writes, and reasons well above his age. He has exceptional concentration, prefers to think problems through carefully, and engages deeply with real-world knowledge (especially space and dinosaurs). He’s organized with his materials and can explain ideas clearly once he’s had time to think.
Weaknesses: He dislikes group work and doesn’t enjoy “teaching” peers; fast, noisy, or performative tasks can frustrate him. He may come off as aloof when he’s actually just focusing, and he’s slower to start if work seems trivial or repetitive.
Home Life: He spends much of his time with his grandmother (a retired library aide) who taught him to read and still tutors him daily, making home routines calm and book-rich. Mum’s hospital-clinic shifts and Dad’s irregular IT contracts mean money can be up-and-down, but the household prioritizes learning—library visits, simple science outings, and consistent study habits.

### The Story:

The meter chirps before the alarm—a tight, electronic hiccup that means we're down to the last few dollars of electricity. I lie still under the thin blanket, eyes tracing the water stain on the ceiling that looks like a map of nowhere. My brother shifts in the other cot, his breathing soft and even, and the radiator hisses once, apologetic. The flat is cold—not freezing, but enough that I can see my breath when I sit up. I reach for my cargo pants, folded on the chair, and dress fast, pulling on yesterday's socks because the clean ones ran out Tuesday.

The bathroom light flickers twice before it holds. I turn the shower tap and wait, hand under the stream, but the water stays cold—the boiler's gone sulky again, or maybe Dad forgot to bleed the line before his night shift. I splash my face, scrub my teeth with the toothpaste squeezed flat, and stare at the kid in the mirror. Brown hair plastered forward, eyes that look older when I'm tired. I don't feel eight. I feel like something wound too tight.

Back in the room, I pull on my faded graphic tee—the one with the bodega cat logo peeling at the edges—and grab the yellow raincoat from the chair. It's there because I put it there last night, before bed, so I wouldn't have to think about it in the morning. The vinyl crinkles when I fold it over my arm.

The kitchen is worse than I expected. Mum stands at the counter in her scrubs—pale blue, creased from yesterday's double shift—phone wedged between her ear and shoulder, staring at the empty breakfast tray. The tray's supposed to have two foil-wrapped plates by now, dropped off by the SprangCare courier, but it's just sitting there, naked and accusing. She doesn't look at me when I come in. Her mouth is a line.

The TV mutters on low volume from the corner of the counter, propped against the toaster. A reporter with helmet hair and a blazer stands outside a brick building I recognize—SprangCare Coventry, where Mum works nights and where my brother gets his inhaler refills. The camera cuts to a man in a dark suit, smiling under a chandelier that looks like it costs more than our building. He's short, a little heavy, and his smile doesn't reach his eyes. Behind him, a banner reads *Umbrella Wellness Weekend* in swooping letters. He poses with a stack of medical-supply boxes, one gloved hand resting on the top carton like he owns it. The reporter's voice drones: *allegations of financial pressure... acquisition... Iceberg Logistics...* I don't understand most of the words, but I understand Mum's face.

"Closed again?" I ask.

She ends the call with a curt "Thanks for nothing" and drops the phone on the counter. "Yeah. Closed. No courier, no breakfast, no—" She stops herself, eyes flicking to my brother, who's padding in barefoot, rubbing his face. She forces a smile. "We'll make do. Cereal bar?"

She digs into the back of the cupboard and pulls out two bars—one for each of us—and a bruised apple that's been sitting in the fruit bowl since last week. She sets them on the counter without ceremony. My stomach growls, but it's not just hunger. It's the other thing. The tightness. The ache that starts low and coils.

I don't ask about the medication. The stool softener comes in the breakfast delivery, tucked into a little paper bag with my name printed on the label. Mum knows I need it. I know she knows. But she's already moving—checking my brother's inhaler, refilling his backpack with the spare, glancing at the clock above the sink. There's no time to call the pharmacy, no time to fix it. I eat the cereal bar slowly, chewing each bite until it's paste, and wash it down with tap water that tastes like the pipes.

My brother slides onto the stool next to me, crunching his bar in three bites. "TV says the Penguin guy bought the clinic."

Mum's shoulders stiffen. "Eat. We're leaving in five."

I don't ask what "bought" means, but I think about the man with the gloved hand on the supply boxes, and I think about the empty tray. My stomach cramps, just a little, like a warning.

I pull the raincoat on. The vinyl is stiff, cold against my skin, and it crinkles when I move. My brother glances over, frowning. "It's not raining."

I zip it up to my chin anyway, tugging the hood cords until they're snug. The coat holds everything in—the smell I'm scared will come, the hunger, the tightness. It's the only thing I can control. I don't answer him. Mum doesn't say anything either. She's already at the door, keys jangling, her face set like stone.

We walk the ten-minute route along the freight wall. The morning is flat gray, no rain but no sun either—just that Gotham in-between that makes everything look like a photograph left too long in a drawer. The El rumbles past overhead, shaking the pavement under our feet, and my brother hops over the cracks, counting switchers on the tracks. I keep my hands in my pockets, feeling the slick lining of the coat, the way it moves when I breathe. My belly feels tight, like something's knotting itself inside. I wonder if I'll make it to lunch. I wonder if anyone will notice.

I think about Henry Point. His breakfast probably showed up. His mum probably didn't stand in the kitchen staring at an empty tray. Henry always looks like he's stepped out of a catalog—blonde hair trimmed neat, polo collar flat, pencil case with every pencil sharpened and every eraser clean. I've seen him at his desk, setting out note cards in perfect stacks, his face calm and serious like he's solving a puzzle only he can see. I don't know what his house looks like, but I bet the lights don't flicker. I bet the boiler works.

At the school gate, I spot him. He's standing near the office steps with his grandmother—a small, tidy woman with gray hair pinned back and a canvas tote slung over one shoulder. She's holding her phone to her ear, her face serious, and Henry stands beside her, still and watchful. He's not fidgeting. He's not whining. He's just *there*, absorbing whatever she's saying, filing it away in that organized brain of his. I can't hear the words, but I see her mouth shape something like "reduced hours" and "supply chain," and Henry nods once, slow, like he's placing the pieces on a mental map.

I turn away before he sees me looking. My jaw sets. I tug the hood tighter.

Mum drops us at the vestibule—kissing my brother's forehead, squeezing my shoulder once, hard—and then she's gone, back toward the El stop and another shift she's not sure will pay. I watch her go, the blue scrubs disappearing into the gray morning, and I feel the raincoat creak around my ribs.

Inside, the hallway gleams. Polished shoes squeak on the tiles, and the "Ready to Learn" posters watch from the walls like judges. I keep my hood up until the teacher at the door gives me *the look*, and then I pull it down, slowly, feeling the air hit my hair. I don't take the coat off. I walk past the check-in nook, past the mood meter with its happy-face magnets, and into Room A-106.

The morning light slants across the circle rug, soft and clean, catching dust motes that drift like snow in a globe. The anchor charts promise a day of "Ready • Kind • Safe," and the visual schedule shows literacy first, then stations, then math. Everything is labeled. Everything is in its place.

Henry is already at his desk, setting out note cards in straight lines, his pencil case squared at the corner. His socks match. His collar sits flat. He doesn't look up when I pass.

I slide into my seat two rows over, raincoat crinkling, and I fold my hands on the desk. My stomach cramps again—small, sharp, a reminder. I press my lips together and stare at the anchor chart, willing the day to start, willing my body to hold, willing the yellow vinyl to do its job.

The teacher smiles at the door, greeting each kid by name, and the room hums with the soft sounds of arrival—zippers, murmurs, the shuffle of chairs. I keep my eyes forward, my jaw tight, and I wait.

___

The teacher gathers us on the circle rug, holding up a picture book with a blue-dressed girl on the cover—Alice, tumbling down a rabbit hole. She explains the interview game in her calm, steady voice: one of us will be Alice, one will be the interviewer, and we'll take turns asking and answering questions about the story. She reads pairs from her clipboard. I'm only half-listening, picking at a thread on my cargo pants, until I hear my name: "Thomas and Henry." My stomach drops. I glance across the rug. Henry Point sits two kids over, blonde hair neat, hands folded in his lap. He's already nodding, like this is no big deal, like he's done a hundred interviews before breakfast.

I swallow hard. The raincoat crinkles when I shift my weight.

"Find your partner and pick a workspace," the teacher says, gesturing to the table pods. "Interviewers, you have five minutes to write three questions. Alices, you'll use that time to think about the story."

The rug erupts into motion—kids pairing off, dragging chairs, giggling. I stand slowly, tugging my raincoat straight, and cross to the table pod by the windows. Henry is already there, pulling out his chair with both hands, settling his pencil case on the desk with a soft *click*. He opens it—mechanical pencil, eraser in a little plastic sleeve, two spare leads in a tiny tube—and sets everything in a row. Then he pulls out a stack of blank index cards from his desk cubby, edges perfectly aligned, and fans them into a neat arc.

I sit down beside him. My chair scrapes, loud, and the raincoat crinkles like a trash bag. I feel my belly cramp again—low, tight, a fist curling inward—and I press my lips together. Henry doesn't look at me. He's already writing on the first card, his pencil moving in smooth, controlled strokes. His handwriting is perfect. Every letter the same height, spacing even, no cross-outs. The card looks like it was printed by a machine.

I watch his hand. Pale skin, clean fingernails, no ink smudges. My own fingers are scuffed, the nails bitten, a faint pencil streak across my thumb from yesterday. I didn't have breakfast. Henry probably had toast and juice. Maybe eggs. Maybe his grandmother made pancakes. I can *see* the difference between us in those neat cards, in the pencil that doesn't wobble, in the collar that sits flat.

"One sec," Henry murmurs, still not looking up. "I'm organising questions."

*Organising*. Like it's a science project. Like we're not just two kids pretending to be Alice and some guy with a clipboard. My chest tightens. The raincoat feels smaller, the vinyl pressing against my ribs, and I want to unzip it, but I can't—I won't. I cross my arms instead and stare at his cards.

Henry finishes the second question, sets it neatly beside the first, and starts on the third. His mechanical pencil makes a soft scratching sound, rhythmic and steady, like a clock ticking. Around us, other pairs are already talking, voices rising and falling, the teacher moving between desks with her clipboard. A girl at the next pod laughs—bright, easy—and I feel the sound like a needle.

Finally, Henry looks up. His blue-gray eyes are serious, calm, like he's about to read the weather forecast. He clears his throat softly and picks up the top card.

"Alice," he says, his voice even and polite, "what did you learn from the bottle marked 'Drink Me'?"

The room goes huge. The other voices blur into a low hum. I feel classmates' eyes flicking toward us—just quick glances, but I *feel* them. My mind is blank. The bottle. The story. I don't remember. I don't *care*. I see Henry's tidy cards and his tidy face and his pencil that writes in perfect lines, and I see the empty breakfast tray on our kitchen counter and the prepay meter beeping its warning and my brother's inhaler rattling in Mum's hand. None of this is *fair*. Henry fits here. The classroom was built for kids like him—kids with breakfasts and pencil cases and grandmothers who walk them to school and don't have to choose between the heating and the pharmacy.

I reach out—fast, before I think—and press my thumb down on the tip of his mechanical pencil.

It snaps. Just a soft *click*, and the lead breaks clean, the black point tumbling onto the desk. Henry's eyes widen. His mouth opens, but no sound comes out.

I pick up the broken lead—sharp, thin, already smudging my fingers—and drag it across the top card. Right through his careful letters. The graphite smears, a thick black streak cutting the question in half. The card is ruined.

"It was nothing," I say. My voice is flat, controlled, like I'm reading an answer off the board.

Henry flinches. It's not a big movement—just a pull-back in his shoulders, a tightening of his mouth—but I see it. I wait for him to yell, to tell the teacher, to shove the card in my face and demand an apology. I *want* him to. I want him to lose that calm, to prove he's not so perfect, to prove he's just a kid like me.

But he doesn't. He just stares at the ruined card, his face going very still. Then he takes a long breath through his nose—the kind Mum does when she's counting to ten—and reaches for a fresh card with careful, deliberate fingers. His jaw is tight. His shoulders are rigid. But he doesn't cry. He doesn't shout. He just sets the new card down and exhales slowly, like he's deflating.

I feel the girl at the next pod staring. When I glance over, she's frozen mid-sentence, eyes wide, and the boy across from her is half-standing, craning to see what happened. The murmur of interviews falters—just a ripple, a pause—and then starts again, quieter, uncertain. The weight of their watching presses on my back, and instead of shame, I feel a strange, bitter satisfaction. I didn't apologize. I *won't*. I sit back in my chair, arms crossed, raincoat creaking like armor.

The teacher is two desks away, crouched beside a pair of girls, nodding as one of them reads a card aloud. But her gaze flicks toward us—just a quick cut of her eyes—and I know she saw. Or heard. Or felt the shift in the room. She straightens slightly, still listening to the girl, but her attention is split now. I meet her eyes for half a second, my face blank and hard, and then I look away.

Henry sets the ruined card aside, face-down, and picks up his mechanical pencil. He clicks it once—a fresh lead slides into place—and writes the question again on the new card, his hand steady. But I see the tension in his fingers, the way his knuckles go white when he presses too hard on a downstroke. I see the line of his mouth, tight and straight. He's not crying, but he's *feeling* something. And that makes me angrier, somehow. Because he gets to feel it quietly, neatly, without spilling over. I feel everything loud and messy and wrong, and he just *breathes* and keeps going.

I stare at the ruined card between us, the black streak like a scar across the words. The raincoat presses against my chest. My belly cramps again, sharper this time, and I shift in my chair, trying to ease the pressure. The room is too bright, too clean, too full of kids who had breakfast and whose pencils don't break and who don't have to wear raincoats on dry days to hold themselves together.

Henry sets the new card down and looks at me. His face is still, controlled, but his eyes are harder now. Not angry—just *aware*. Like he's solved a problem and filed it away.

"Alice," he says again, his voice a little quieter, "what did you learn from the bottle marked 'Drink Me'?"

I stare at him. My jaw sets. I don't answer.

The teacher's footsteps squeak on the tile, coming closer.

___

The teacher stops beside our desk, her shadow falling across the ruined card. I brace—shoulders tight, jaw set, raincoat creaking—waiting for the lecture, the disappointed voice, the walk of shame to the principal's office. But she doesn't raise her voice. She crouches so she's eye-level with both of us, and her face is calm, serious. Not angry. Just *there*. She glances at the card, at Henry's stiff shoulders, at my crossed arms. Then she straightens and says to the room, not loud but clear enough that everyone hears: "Scripts down. Eyes on your own text. We protect work."

The hum of interviews falters. Pencils pause. Eyes flick toward us, then away. She doesn't wait for acknowledgment—just turns back to Henry, voice low and even. "Continue from card two. Come to the teacher table—I'll get you fresh materials."

Henry nods once, face still and composed, and gathers his cards in a neat stack. He doesn't look at me. Doesn't glare, doesn't cry, doesn't say a word. He just stands, chair sliding back with barely a sound, and walks to the teacher's table at the front of the room. His shoulders are tight, but his steps are steady. I watch him go, and something in my chest twists—not guilt, not yet, but something close. Disappointment, maybe. That he didn't crack.

The teacher waits until Henry's seated, then turns back to me. She doesn't crouch again; she stands, hands loose at her sides, and looks at me with those steady, serious eyes. "Rough morning," she says quietly. Not a question. A fact. "That move wasn't okay."

Not *you're bad*. Not *you're a problem*. Just: *that wasn't okay*. I stare at the desk, at the ruined card lying face-up between us, the black streak bisecting Henry's careful letters. My jaw is clenched so tight my teeth ache.

She points to the supply tub at the back of the room. "Fix the harm. Rewrite Henry's marked card—use your neatest letters—and replace the lead. There's a pack in the blue bin."

I want to argue. I want to say *He doesn't need my help* or *It's just a card* or *None of this is fair anyway*, but her eyes are waiting, patient and immovable, and the words stick in my throat. I nod, stiff, barely a dip of my chin.

"Good," she says. Then she leans in, just a fraction, and adds, "Then you're Stage Manager for the pair interviews. Set two stools by the rug, keep time with the sand timer, and help pairs transition. I need someone who notices details."

Stage Manager. Not time-out. Not sitting in the Calm Corner staring at breathing cards. A *job*. I glance up, surprised, and catch the edge of something in her face—not pity, not disappointment, just... expectation. Like she's handing me a tool and trusting me to use it.

"If you feel that burn again," she says, turning to walk back to the front, "reset or tell me, 'Not now—later.' I'll nod. We'll handle it."

She doesn't wait for me to answer. She's already moving, calling the next pair of names, her voice cutting through the low hum of the room like a conductor tapping a baton. I sit for a second longer, the raincoat pressing tight against my ribs, and then I stand.

The supply tub sits on a low shelf by the bookcase, plastic bins sorted by color and labeled with pictures and words: *Paper*, *Pencils*, *Glue*, *Extras*. I find a blank index card in the green bin and a sharpened pencil in the red caddy. The blue bin holds a small plastic case of mechanical pencil leads—thin, black, lined up like tiny soldiers. I take one refill, pinch it between my fingers, and carry everything to an empty corner of a table pod where no one's sitting.

I set the card on a clipboard and stare at it. Blank. Clean. Waiting. I pick up the pencil and start copying Henry's question in my best handwriting. My letters aren't as neat as his—my *t*s tilt a little, and the spacing isn't perfect—but they're clear. Readable. I press down harder than I need to, the graphite biting into the card, and when I'm done I set the pencil down and flex my hand. My fingers ache.

I walk back to Henry's desk—he's still at the teacher's table, head bent over a fresh stack of cards, pencil moving in smooth, controlled strokes—and set the new card in the center of his workspace. Then I press the lead refill into the barrel of his mechanical pencil, clicking it once to test. A fresh point slides out, sharp and black. I leave the pencil beside the card.

The ruined card goes in the scrap bin, face-down, buried under torn worksheet edges and shavings from the pencil sharpener. I don't look at it. I walk back to the front of the room.

The teacher is standing by the circle rug, hands loose at her sides, waiting for the room to settle. When the last pair finishes their murmured prep, she lifts her chin and speaks, her voice calm but clear.

"Listen up. Our norm: we restore and move on. Interviews are about *ideas*, not perfect cards. If you make a mistake—rip a page, snap a lead, say something sharp—we fix it and keep going. That's what learning looks like."

She doesn't look at me. Doesn't say my name. Doesn't point. But the words land in my chest like stones dropping into water. Around the room, kids nod. A few glance at me—quick, cautious—and then turn back to their desks. The girl at the next pod picks up her script again, whispering to her partner, and the hum of work resumes.

The teacher waves me over. She hands me a sand timer—smooth glass, heavy in my palm, the sand pooled at the bottom like gold dust—and gestures to two stools stacked by the bookshelf. "Set them by the rug, facing each other. When a pair finishes, flip the timer and call the next names from the list. You're the keeper of the flow."

I take the timer carefully, cradling it in both hands, and walk to the bookshelf. The stools are light but awkward, legs jutting at angles, and I lift them one at a time, carrying them to the rug. I set them down facing each other, adjusting them so they're straight, so the legs don't wobble. The movement feels good—purposeful, clear. My hands have something to do. My body has a place to be. I'm not just sitting with the burn in my chest, the tightness in my belly, the weight of the raincoat pressing in. I'm *doing* something.

I flip the sand timer, watching the first grains tumble through the narrow waist, and call the first pair of names from the teacher's list. Two kids hop up from their desks, grinning, and settle on the stools. One of them—a girl with braids and a gap-tooth smile—waves her script like a flag. "I'm the Mad Hatter!"

"Go ahead," I say, my voice flat but steady. I step to the side, arms crossed, and watch the sand trickle down.

At the teacher's table, Henry finishes another card and adds it to his stack. His face is calm again, shoulders loose, and when the teacher leans over to murmur something, he nods and picks up his pencil. He looks... fine. Not upset. Not gloating. Just *fine*. It's frustrating—part of me still wants him to show something, to prove I got to him—but it's also something else. He didn't fall apart. I didn't break him. And the teacher didn't break me. We're both still here.

The sand runs out. I flip the timer, call the next pair, and the stools fill again. This time it's two boys, one bouncing on his seat, the other reading his card like it's a treaty. I keep my eyes on the timer, on the steady stream of falling sand, and I breathe.

The raincoat is still zipped. My belly is still tight. The morning—empty tray, cold water, the meter's warning chirp—still sits heavy in my chest. But the room feels a little less huge. A little less like I'm drowning. The stools are straight. The timer is ticking. I'm holding the flow.

Across the room, Henry sets down his pencil and stretches his fingers, rolling his shoulders once. He doesn't look at me. I don't look at him. But we're both working. Both here. Both still standing.

The teacher circulates, clipboard in hand, pausing to listen to interviews, to nod, to ask a follow-up question. When she passes me, she doesn't stop, doesn't check in, doesn't hover. She just glances at the timer, at the neat line of waiting pairs, and gives a single, small nod.

I nod back.

The sand keeps falling.
